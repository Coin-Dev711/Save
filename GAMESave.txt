Enemy AI potential stuff:
--Core Structure: State Machines
Most AI use Finite State Machines (FSMs) with states like Idle, Patrol, Chase, Attack, and Flee. Transitions are triggered by player actions, distance, or health thresholds.
-- Perception: How Enemies Detect Players:
Vision cones: Field-of-view checks (like 120° forward).
Line of sight: Raycasting to check visibility.
Hearing: Alerted by noise (sprinting, combat).
Detection timers: Gradual awareness to avoid instant aggro.
--Decision Making
Basic enemies use scripted logic or FSMs.
Advanced enemies (or bosses) use Behavior Trees or Decision Trees for more flexible choices.
Some include cooldowns, randomness, and condition checks (e.g., “If player behind and low HP, retreat.”).
--Movement and Navigation
Use navmeshes or pathfinding (A*).
Can include flanking, retreating, or avoiding hazards.
Often animation-driven to keep movement natural.
--Combat Behavior
Attack patterns: Predefined move sets (telegraphed swings, combos).
Windups/delays: Adds mind games and challenge.
Phases: Bosses change behavior at HP thresholds.
--Coordination
Group AI can alert others, take positions, or wait turns.
Some games assign roles: tank, ranged, support, etc.
--Reactions and Feedback
React to hits: stagger, flee.
Dynamic difficulty: increase aggression over time or based on skill.
--Optimization
Use culling to disable distant AI.
Level of Detail (LOD) for behavior complexity.
Tick throttling: Only update AI every few frames.

For ranged energy moves to be controlled, you need a connection to your main energy source. It’s similar to how computers must be connected to interact with each other.
When you launch a blast, it’s still made of your energy, but to control it after release, you must stay linked to it. One way to do this is by maintaining a small “energy thread” between you and the blast. This thread keeps you connected so you can continue to influence it. Without that connection, you can’t control the move, because you’d have no way to interact with it once it’s left you. Of course, this is just one way to maintain control—there could be others. But the basic idea is that controlling a ranged move requires range: if your energy link can’t reach the blast, you won’t be able to affect it anymore.

A long-running series can feel fresh even with a repeating formula by changing the details while keeping the core structure. You can raise the stakes or expand the world each installment, introduce new rules or power systems, and shift the focus to different characters to create new emotional angles. Varying the tone or lightly changing genres keeps familiar beats from feeling repetitive. Distinct villains with unique motives and philosophies reshape the story’s dynamics, while small but meaningful character growth alters how recurring events play out. Expanding the universe—new locations, histories, or cosmic layers—adds novelty without replacing the formula. Finally, occasionally subverting expectations, such as letting a different character resolve the climax or altering a standard beat, prevents predictability. These techniques let each installment follow the same structure while still offering new experiences.
If a human’s brain, body, and consciousness all sped up proportionally, the usual lag between thought, perception, and action would shrink, letting reflexes, awareness, and movement occur almost simultaneously. External events would appear slower, and perception would become hyper-detailed, revealing subtleties normally filtered out. The main downsides would be psychological—information overload, temporal distortion, and sensory stress—but these would not be directly deadly if the body could physically handle the speed.
